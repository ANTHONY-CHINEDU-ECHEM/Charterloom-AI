# Scaled Agile Release Train

## When to use it
A release train aligns several agile teams, usually four to twelve, around a shared mission, a shared cadence and a single programme backlog. It is appropriate when no single team can deliver meaningful value alone and when cross team dependencies are the dominant schedule risk. Charters that describe multi team programmes, platform teams supporting product teams, or many business owners sharing outcomes point to this archetype.

## Lifecycle and states
Programme Backlog, Feature Analysis, Committed to Increment, Feature In Progress, System Demo, Integration Testing, Release Ready and Released, with Dependency Blocked and Deferred as side states.

## Planning increments
Work is planned in programme increments of eight to twelve weeks containing several two week iterations. Increment planning brings every team together to commit to objectives, surface dependencies and agree risks. Features are prioritised by weighted shortest job first, which divides the cost of delay by job size.

## Dependency management
A dependency board shows which team needs what from whom and by when. Items blocked on another team move to Dependency Blocked with a named dependency owner. Blocked dependencies older than two days escalate automatically to the release train engineer and the dependency owner. A weekly coordination sync reviews the board.

## Risk management
Programme risks are classified as resolved, owned, accepted or mitigated, often called ROAM. Labelling a work item as a risk should create a record on the ROAM board.

## Metrics
Increment predictability, the ratio of achieved to planned business value; feature cycle time; open dependency count; release frequency.

## Signals of a good fit in a charter
Four or more teams, several locations, platform and product teams contributing to the same release, a programme office, many stakeholder departments and a duration beyond six months.

## Common failure modes
Running a train for two teams adds ceremony without benefit. Increment planning that ignores capacity creates commitments the teams cannot meet. Treating the train as a reporting structure rather than an integration mechanism leaves integration to the end, where it fails.
